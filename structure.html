How a T cell reads a cell's interior

Every nucleated cell in your body continuously shreds a sample of its own proteins and displays the fragments on its surface. This is the machine that does the displaying — and the tumour fragment it is holding up. Scroll to move through the structure; drag to look around.

01 · The complex

A molecular display case

This is a peptide–MHC class I complex: three chains that only make sense together. A long heavy chain does the presenting, a small light chain holds it stable, and clamped between them sits a ten-residue fragment of a protein from inside the cell.

Passing T cells inspect it. If the fragment looks foreign — viral, or mutated — the cell is marked for destruction.

02 · The groove

Eight strands and two helices

The α1 and α2 domains of the heavy chain fold into one continuous platform: a floor of eight antiparallel β-strands, walled by two long α-helices. The cleft between those helices is the peptide-binding groove.

Unlike class II, this groove is closed at both ends. That is why class I peptides are short — typically eight to ten residues, pinned at the termini and bulging in the middle.

03 · The scaffold

β2-microglobulin, underneath

The small chain below never touches the peptide. It is invariant — the same 100-residue immunoglobulin fold on every class I molecule you make — and it binds non-covalently beneath the platform.

Without it the heavy chain does not fold correctly and never reaches the cell surface. Cells that lose it go effectively invisible to cytotoxic T cells, which is one route by which tumours escape.

04 · The T cell's view

Looking down into the cleft

This is roughly what an approaching T-cell receptor sees: two helical ridges, and the peptide lying between them. The receptor reads both at once — part MHC surface, part peptide — which is why recognition is restricted to a particular HLA type.

Only the upward-facing side chains are legible. The rest of the peptide is buried in the pockets below.

05 · The epitope

ELAGLGINTV

A decapeptide from MART-1 / Melan-A, a protein made by melanocytes and by most melanomas. Its second residue (leucine) and its last (valine) drop into the B and F pockets of the groove — the canonical anchor preferences of HLA-A*0201, the most common class I allele in people of European descent.

That combination made this epitope one of the most intensively studied targets in cancer immunotherapy. The peptide crystallised here is an engineered variant, modified to sit in the groove more tightly than the natural sequence does.

About this model

Geometry generated directly from PDB 3MRP — Crystal structure of MHC class I HLA-A2 complexed with a Melan-A/MART-1 decapeptide variant. The heavy chain (chain A, 275 residues, HLA-A*0201) and β2-microglobulin (chain B, 100 residues) are drawn as cartoon ribbons built by splining the Cα trace, with helices and β-strands taken from the deposited secondary structure records. The peptide (chain P) is drawn in ball-and-stick. Waters are omitted.

The ribbons, spheres and bonds were tessellated offline, packed into a single glTF binary with three named nodes, and compressed with meshopt — about 293 KB in total, fetched only once you scroll near it. The heavy chain carries the A245V substitution present in the deposited construct.
