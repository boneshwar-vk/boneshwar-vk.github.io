How a neural text-to-speech system turns text into sound

Pipeline 0%

01 Text

A sentence, before it is sound

Everything a speech model produces begins as characters. This sentence is drawn from about twenty thousand points — and every one of them stays with us to the end.

02 Tokenisation

Seven pieces, not four words

The text is split into sub-word units: The fut ure is spo ken .

Splitting below the word is what lets a model pronounce something it has never seen before.

03 Embedding

Each token becomes a vector

Every token is mapped to a point in a learned space where distance carries meaning. Tokens keep their colour here — the cluster that was spo is still spo.

A real embedding has hundreds of dimensions. This is three of them, arranged for legibility — a projection, not the space itself.

04 Encoder

Four layers, all-to-all

The vectors enter a stack of transformer layers, drawn along the depth axis. Within each layer every token can read every other token — the lines are that connectivity, not learned weights.

05 Attention

Context decides delivery

Self-attention lets each token incorporate context from the whole sentence. Here spo is the query, and the beams show what it draws on — thickness and brightness scale with weight.

This is why the same word is not spoken the same way twice: how spoken is realised depends on what came before it.

Weights are illustrative, not read from a trained model.

06 Latent

Linguistic, not yet acoustic

What leaves the encoder is no longer text and not yet sound: a dense representation carrying who said what, how fast, and with what emphasis. The camera passes through it.

07 Acoustic

A mel spectrogram

The decoder predicts a spectrogram: how much energy sits at each frequency, moment by moment. The ridges running left to right are formants — the resonances that make a vowel that vowel.

Real surface: a 1024-point STFT of a formant-synthesised utterance, on a mel scale. Heights are measured, not drawn.

08 Waveform

The vocoder collapses it to a signal

The frequency axis folds away and what is left is pressure over time — the thing a speaker can actually move air with.

“The future is spoken.” — 1.31 s at 22.05 kHz.

About this visualisation

The scene is one particle system, not eight illustrations. The same points that spell the sentence become the token clusters, the projected embedding field, the nodes of the encoder stack, the latent cloud, the ridges of the spectrogram, and finally the waveform. Each point keeps its token identity the whole way through, which is the only reason the transitions mean anything.

Geometry is generated in the browser: the letterforms are sampled from glyphs rasterised on a canvas, and every other layout is computed. Nothing is a downloaded model. Positions for two adjacent stages live in vertex attributes and a single uniform crossfades between them, so scrolling costs one uniform write per frame rather than a geometry rebuild.

The acoustic scenes are the one part with real numbers underneath. A small Klatt-style formant synthesiser renders the sentence from a phoneme script — glottal source, three formant resonators, fricative noise — and the surface is a genuine short-time Fourier transform of that signal on a mel scale. The waveform is the same signal. Nothing plays; the audio exists only as data.

The encoder, embedding and attention scenes are conceptual by contrast, and the captions say so where it matters. They show the shape of the computation, not the parameters of any particular model.
